# Tomato Disease Management Guide

## Tomato Bacterial Spot
Cause: The bacterium Xanthomonas, spread by splashing water, infected seed and handling wet plants.
Symptoms: Small dark water-soaked spots on leaves that turn brown with a yellow halo. Raised scabby
spots appear on the fruit, making it unsellable. Severe infection causes heavy leaf drop.
Treatment: Spray copper hydroxide or copper oxychloride at 2 to 3 grams per litre of water every
7 to 10 days. Tank mixing copper with mancozeb improves control. There is no cure once the plant is
infected, so the goal is to protect healthy leaves. Remove and destroy badly infected plants.
Prevention: Use certified disease-free seed. Treat seed with hot water at 50 degrees Celsius for
25 minutes before sowing. Rotate away from tomato, chilli and capsicum for two years. Avoid working
in the field when the foliage is wet. Use drip irrigation instead of overhead sprinklers.

## Tomato Early Blight
Cause: The fungus Alternaria solani. It survives in old crop debris and soil, and attacks older
lower leaves first, especially on plants stressed by poor nutrition or heavy fruit load.
Symptoms: Brown spots with concentric rings that look like a target or bullseye, surrounded by a
yellow area. Lower leaves yellow and fall off first, then the damage moves upward. Dark sunken
lesions can form on the stem and at the stem end of the fruit.
Treatment: Spray mancozeb 75 percent WP at 2 grams per litre, or chlorothalonil at 2 grams per litre,
at 7 to 10 day intervals starting when the first spots appear. Azoxystrobin or difenoconazole can be
used as an alternative, and rotating between different chemical groups prevents resistance.
Remove and burn infected lower leaves. Do not compost them.
Prevention: Stake or trellis plants so air moves freely. Mulch the soil to stop rain splashing spores
onto the lowest leaves. Keep nitrogen adequate, since weak plants are hit harder. Rotate crops
for two to three years and plough in debris after harvest.

## Tomato Late Blight
Cause: The oomycete Phytophthora infestans. This is the most destructive tomato disease and it moves
extremely fast, destroying a whole field in under a week in cool wet weather.
Symptoms: Large greasy grey-green or brown patches on leaves, usually starting at the leaf edge or
tip. A white fuzzy mould appears on the underside in humid conditions. Stems get dark brown streaks
and fruit develops firm greasy brown blotches. The crop has a distinct rotting smell.
Treatment: Act immediately, this is an emergency. Spray metalaxyl plus mancozeb at 2 grams per litre,
or cymoxanil plus mancozeb at 3 grams per litre. Follow up with a protectant such as mancozeb or
chlorothalonil every 5 to 7 days while cool humid weather continues. Uproot and burn severely
infected plants right away so they do not act as a source for the rest of the field.
Prevention: Late blight thrives at 15 to 22 degrees Celsius with high humidity, fog or continuous
rain, so begin protective sprays before those conditions arrive. Give wide plant spacing, avoid
evening irrigation so leaves dry before nightfall, and never plant tomatoes next to potatoes since
the same pathogen attacks both.

## Healthy Tomato Plant Care
If no disease is detected, the plant is healthy and no pesticide should be applied. Spraying a
healthy crop wastes money, harms beneficial insects and speeds up pesticide resistance.
Maintenance: Keep soil pH between 6.0 and 6.8. Water at the base with 25 to 40 mm per week,
preferably by drip, keeping moisture even to prevent blossom end rot and fruit cracking.
Mulch with straw or plastic to hold moisture and block soil-borne spores. Stake plants and prune
the lower leaves that touch the soil. Apply a balanced NPK fertiliser and add calcium if blossom
end rot has been a problem. Scout the field twice a week and look under the leaves, since catching
a disease in the first few plants is far cheaper than treating a whole field.
